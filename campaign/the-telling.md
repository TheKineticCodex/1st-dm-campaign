# THE SONG THE SEA FORGOT — The Telling
### The same story, told as a story. For reading aloud, or for a player who asks.

*The formal version, with every decision and table procedure, is
`the-story.md`. This is the one you'd tell by a fire.*

---

## What a song is

Every living thing hums a little tune it never learned. Not out loud — under
everything. It's the sound of what the thing *is*. The Sea's tune goes:
*I am the one who loves the Moon, and I rise every night to reach her.* A
grove's tune goes: *I bloom.* A person's tune is whoever they are when
nobody's watching.

We call that tune a **song.**

Here is the thing nobody tells you about songs: **they don't stay in one
place.** When you love something, a piece of its song moves into you. That's
just what love does. Your mother's song is in you. Your home's song is in
you. And everything ever born inside the Sea — every mermaid, every seal,
one particular alligator — was born humming a piece of the Sea's.

So if you ever wanted to *own* a song — the whole thing, all of it — you
couldn't just take it. You'd have to go to everyone who ever loved the
thing, one at a time, and get each of them to hand their piece over. And
they'd have to *mean* it.

There are people who do exactly that.

## The Ones Below

Down in the dark there's a market. The fairies who keep it don't sell things
— they *keep* them. Not gold. The selves of things: a name, a spring, a
face, a laugh, a promise, a song. Shelf after shelf, each one humming softly,
each one something that used to know what it was and doesn't anymore.

They are very polite. They are never late. They have never told a lie. And
they have never, not once, stolen anything.

They don't need to. **They get you to say yes.**

They know exactly what you think you'll never miss, and they price it. They
show up on the worst night of your life. And they put the whole offer
inside a carnival, so that saying yes feels like winning a game.

And in the old places, a yes is not just a word. Say *yes, take it* and mean
it — and the thing leaves you. Nobody comes to collect. It just goes, the
way a stone goes when you let it fall.

But there is one thing even they can't get around. **You can only give
away what's yours.** Promise away something that other people hold pieces
of, and the promise has a crack in it. The fairies will take it anyway —
they'll keep it happily on the shelf for a thousand years. But it doesn't
*hold.* The day the people who really own it come and stand together and
ask for it back, it goes home. It has to. That's the crack.

You'll want to remember that.

## The Sea and the Moon

Long ago, the Sea was in love with the Moon.

It sang to her every night — the one thing it touches every day and can
never reach. The tides were the Sea rising up to get closer. The song was
what pulled them. And once in a very long while, the Moon came *near* — low
and huge and close enough that the Sea could almost believe — and then she
went away again, the way she always does.

After one of those nights, the Sea couldn't bear it anymore.

It went down to the market in the dark and it said: *Take the song. Take
the part of me that remembers her. I don't want it.* And the fairies said
yes, because they always say yes. And the Sea forgot.

That's how you give a song away. You don't hand over a tune. You agree to
stop being the one who carries it. The Sea is still there — still wet,
still moving — but it doesn't know *why* it moves anymore. It stopped
rising to reach her. And a sea that's forgotten why it moves starts, very
slowly, to shrink. Ask the lighthouse-keeper. Eleven feet of horizon gone
in one lifetime.

But the Sea couldn't give it *all* away. It was never only the Sea's. Every
creature born inside it still carried a piece — and the fairies knew it.
So they've spent every century since going creature to creature, buying up
the pieces, one yes at a time. A mermaid here. An old fortune-teller there.
Anyone born of salt water who could be talked into forgetting.

There is one piece left. A girl named Peaches carries it. She has never
said yes.

## Forty years ago, and now

Forty years ago, the Moon came near again. There was a boy in Saltmere,
eight years old, who saw lights out on the sand where the water should have
been. By dawn the tide was back, and it had taken his chalk drawing off the
cellar wall. He tore that night out of his father's tide-book so he'd stop
looking at it. He's the harbormaster now. He'll tell you, if you ask him
straight.

The Moon is coming near again.

That's why there's a carnival standing on the seabed. That's why the tide
went out and didn't come back — nobody stole it, nobody's holding it. The
carnival carries the Sea's own song in its heart, in the organ of an old
carousel, and when it grounded on the sand the water drained toward the
thing it once gave away and can't remember, and pressed up against the fence,
grieving, without knowing why. There's a little girl on Net Row who has it
exactly right: *the tide's not lost. It went to the party. It'll come home
when the party's over.*

And every night from now until it's over, the Moon gets a little bigger. A
little wronger. Everyone in Saltmere is going to stop saying so.

## The Five

Five strangers came to Saltmere on one tide. Each of them is the last one
carrying a song that somebody, somewhere, gave away.

**Peaches** carries the Sea's last note. She walks on borrowed feet — she
never said yes; the fairies came for what they said was theirs and she woke
on the shore with legs — and her alligator, Pookie, saw it happen. He
followed her out of the water and has never left her side. He runs off when
he's worried. Watch for that.

**Freya Moon** carries the last blossom of a grove that went quiet — her
home's song, in a crystal at her throat. Her older sister Elowen taught her
to speak to flowers, and told her once that kindness could be stronger than
magic, and was never found. The blossom stirs when it's near anything taken.
It's going to lead her straight to her sister.

**Billy** carries his own song, and doesn't know it. There's a scholar named
William who stays up reading and secretly writes songs about his friends,
and there's a showman named Blue-Chew Billy who jokes whenever anything gets
too close — and under both of them, the Feywild took his true name, and it
sits on a shelf in the dark, humming. He's afraid the person everyone loves
isn't really him. His name is hidden inside the songs he wrote about the
people at this table. They're already carrying him. They just don't know it.

**Philip** carries his mother's song — a tune she hummed in her garden that
he can't quite call back anymore. He was raised to kill and chose to be
gentle instead, and he holds that line every single day, and he was
following a teacher who preached peace and taught the opposite: the shape
of a man who sold his convictions in the dark and kept walking. Philip
won't kill. He's the only fighter at this table built for a war you can't
win by fighting.

**Freya Sun** is the one they haven't got. Her song is whole. She wears
her armor on the outside — black wings, two swords, a face that says don't
— so nobody checks for the wound. She has a list of people she'd die for,
and Peaches and Philip are on it. The fairies want her song more than any of
the others, and they will never come for her. They'll come for her list,
and let her love do the rest.

The gatekeepers put it best, on their slate, the night the five arrived:
*ADMISSION IS SOLD IN SETS. YOU FIVE ARRIVED ON ONE TIDE.*

The fairies gathered them as a collection. They'll leave as a chord.

## What happens

They walk into the carnival, and pay the toll — one true answer each, out
loud, written down. They lose a game and win a page in a dead man's
handwriting. An old woman with gills reads them their own secrets back. A
carousel plays a song with a hole in it, and if the wrong girl hums along,
every lantern in the place turns to look at her.

Then the back fence becomes three gates, and one opens tonight, and the
others remember being chosen last.

Beyond the gates: a bog where a woman keeps voices in green jars. A hall
where a man keeps everyone's finest hour on loop. A drowned market where a
mermaid's tail hangs in a window, priced *one song, already paid.* On each
road there's someone to free and a piece of a song to bring home — and
somewhere on the first road, when they aren't looking for it, two pieces
of a song will be near each other and *pull.* A jar will hum back. A pendant
will warm. Something small will unlatch itself. And nobody will explain it.

Somewhere in the middle, the politest man they've ever met will pour them
tea and tell them the truth: *The Sea wasn't robbed. It asked us. It wanted
to forget her. Who are you to give it back its grief?* And then he'll
leave, and they'll have to decide what they think about that.

And on the night the Moon is closest — huge, silver, close enough that the
Sea is standing up on its hind legs to reach her — they'll stand on the
carnival's biggest stage with everyone they freed along the way, and the
gatekeepers will read back their five true answers, and the five of them
will sing. Each their own song. Five songs, one chord.

And the Sea will remember her. And choose to keep remembering. And the tide
will come home up Saltmere harbor at dawn — *when the party's over* — and
the chapel bell will ring by itself, and the horizon will step back eleven
feet.

Everything comes home. Not everyone stays. A grove blooms. A dead man's
pages rewrite themselves in his own hand, with one new page addressed to a
boy he knew before. A tune comes back to a gentle man whole. A woman with
black wings finds out she can be protected too. And a girl gets her tail
back — and goes home to her coral house, because she said she would — and
an alligator who has never spoken says one thing, once, to the people she
leaves behind:

*"She sang to me first."*

The polite man survives. He always does. He keeps everything that was
freely given, and files the rest under *instructive*, and leaves whoever
out-sang him a card, and a real compliment: *"We'll be watching your career
with interest."*

That's the story. Now go tell it.
